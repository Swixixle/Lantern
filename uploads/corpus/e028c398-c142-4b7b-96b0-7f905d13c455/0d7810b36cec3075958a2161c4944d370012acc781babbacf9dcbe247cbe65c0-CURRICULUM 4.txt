Agathakathological Governance in Stratified Recursive Epistemology

Applications, Architectures, and Institutional Implications

A Capstone Paper

⸻

Abstract

This paper introduces agathakathological epistemology as a necessary ethical substrate for Stratified Recursive Epistemology (SRE)—a framework designed to manage knowledge production, evaluation, and execution across layered domains of consequence. Whereas traditional epistemology seeks either certainty or probabilistic optimization, agathakathological epistemology begins from the premise that all epistemic acts are intrinsically double-edged: simultaneously productive and harmful, enabling and distorting.

We argue that modern socio-technical systems—particularly AI-mediated decision infrastructures—require epistemic frameworks that do not merely minimize error, but bind inevitable harm to accountability, traceability, and refusal mechanisms. We demonstrate how agathakathological principles are operationalized within a concrete architectural ecosystem comprising the Epistemic Liability Index (ELI), Lantern (a guided reasoning interface), the War Room (institutional adjudication space), and LOGE/Nikodemus (a governed execution engine).

The paper concludes that agathakathological SRE represents a shift from outcome-oriented intelligence to process-oriented moral containment, redefining refusal, delay, and friction as epistemic virtues rather than system failures.

⸻

1. Introduction: The Failure of Harm-Blind Epistemology

Contemporary epistemic systems—ranging from academic research pipelines to AI decision engines—are overwhelmingly optimized for accuracy, speed, and utility. While such optimization has yielded unprecedented productive capacity, it has simultaneously generated a crisis of legitimacy: decisions are made without durable records of why alternatives were refused, harms were accepted, or uncertainties were overridden.

Classical epistemology treats harm as external to knowing. Applied epistemology treats harm as a risk to be minimized. Neither is adequate for systems whose outputs directly enact legal, medical, economic, or geopolitical consequences.

This paper proposes that epistemology itself must internalize harm—not as noise, but as structure.

We introduce agathakathological epistemology (from agathos and kakos, good and bad) as a framework in which every epistemic act is understood as morally entangled, and therefore must be governed rather than merely evaluated. When integrated into Stratified Recursive Epistemology (SRE), this framework enables systems that do not promise correctness, but instead promise containment, accountability, and refusal with record.

⸻

2. Stratified Recursive Epistemology (SRE)

2.1 Stratification: Knowledge Across Layers of Consequence

SRE begins with the observation that not all knowledge claims occupy the same ethical or institutional space. It therefore introduces epistemic stratification, typically including:
	•	Layer 0 – Data / Sensory Input
	•	Layer 1 – Interpretation / Modeling
	•	Layer 2 – Actionable Claims
	•	Layer 3 – Institutional Consequence
	•	Layer 4 – Meta-Epistemic Reflection

A claim may be epistemically acceptable at one layer and morally unacceptable at another. Traditional systems collapse these distinctions, allowing interpretive artifacts to propagate unchecked into action.

2.2 Recursion: Knowledge Re-Evaluated Under Escalating Stakes

Recursive epistemology requires that claims re-justify themselves as they traverse layers. Each recursion increases epistemic burden: more evidence, stricter constraints, and clearer provenance are demanded as consequence rises.

However, recursion alone is insufficient. Without an ethical frame, recursion becomes optimization theater—more checks, but no principled stopping condition.

⸻

3. Agathakathology: Ethics for Epistemic Harm

3.1 Definition

Agathakathological epistemology holds that:

Every epistemic act simultaneously produces good and harm; ethical knowing consists not in eliminating harm, but in choosing, bounding, and recording which harm is accepted.

This rejects both moral purification (the fantasy of harmless knowledge) and relativism (the abdication of responsibility).

3.2 From Error Minimization to Harm Selection

In agathakathological systems, the central question shifts from:
	•	“Is this claim true?”
to
	•	“Which harm are we accepting by allowing this claim to advance—and why this one?”

False approval is treated as catastrophic; false refusal is treated as costly but recoverable. This asymmetric valuation is foundational.

⸻

4. Agathakathological SRE: The Combined Framework

When agathakathology is embedded into SRE, the result is a system with three defining properties:
	1.	Layer-Sensitive Harm Tolerance
What is acceptable speculation at low layers becomes unacceptable risk at higher ones.
	2.	Recursive Moral Gating
Each epistemic elevation triggers a reassessment of harm vectors.
	3.	Refusal as Epistemic Output
Refusal is not absence of knowledge, but a positive epistemic artifact with explanatory force.

⸻

5. Architectural Instantiations

5.1 LOGE / Nikodemus: Governed Execution as Moral Enforcement

LOGE (the Logic of Governed Execution), implemented concretely in the Nikodemus engine, is the enforcement core of agathakathological SRE.

Key features include:
	•	Proposal–Execution Separation
Claims are proposed, frozen, and hashed before execution is permitted.
	•	Drift Detection (Deterministic and Semantic)
Any deviation—lexical or semantic—constitutes a new epistemic act requiring re-authorization.
	•	Replay Protection and Token Burning
Each permission is single-use, emphasizing irreversibility and responsibility.
	•	Immutable Receipts
Every attempt, including refusals, generates a durable audit artifact.

Nikodemus embodies agathakathology by making refusal irreversible, legible, and accountable.

⸻

5.2 Lantern: Human-Legible Epistemic Navigation

Lantern is the interface layer of SRE. Where Nikodemus enforces, Lantern explains.

Lantern operationalizes agathakathology by:
	•	Making refusals intelligible rather than opaque
	•	Showing which epistemic layer blocked progression
	•	Exposing the tradeoffs between speed, certainty, and harm

Lantern does not override governance; it renders governance comprehensible. In doing so, it converts epistemic frustration into epistemic trust.

⸻

5.3 The War Room: Institutional Deliberation Under Constraint

The War Room represents the collective epistemic layer—where multiple stakeholders confront the same refusal or risk boundary.

Within an agathakathological SRE, the War Room serves to:
	•	Deliberate on whether a refused claim should be revised or abandoned
	•	Explicitly negotiate which harm is acceptable under new constraints
	•	Generate revised proposals with higher epistemic burden

Crucially, the War Room cannot bypass Nikodemus; it must persuade the system by changing the claim, not the rules.

⸻

5.4 ELI: Quantifying Epistemic Liability

The Epistemic Liability Index (ELI) provides a quantitative complement to agathakathological reasoning.

ELI does not measure “truth.”
It measures downstream exposure: legal, ethical, social, and systemic harm if a claim is wrong or misused.

In SRE:
	•	ELI thresholds determine whether a claim may advance layers
	•	Rising ELI demands stricter recursion and stronger evidence
	•	High-ELI domains (medicine, law, finance) default toward refusal

ELI thus formalizes the agathakathological insight that some knowledge is too dangerous to move quickly.

⸻

6. Refusal as a First-Class Epistemic Act

Traditional epistemology treats refusal as epistemic failure. Agathakathological SRE treats refusal as:
	•	A moral act
	•	A stabilizing force
	•	A recordable outcome with justificatory content

A refusal answers a real question:

“Why did we choose not to know or act yet?”

In systems of power, this answer is often more important than the action itself.

⸻

7. Implications and Risks

7.1 Benefits
	•	Increased institutional trust
	•	Reduced catastrophic error
	•	Clearer accountability chains
	•	Resistance to manipulation and drift

7.2 Costs
	•	Slower decision cycles
	•	Increased friction
	•	Frustration for actors accustomed to permissive systems

These costs are not bugs; they are the price of moral containment.

7.3 The Central Danger

An agathakathological system can be misused as a tool of obstruction or control if refusal power is centralized without transparency. This is why receipts, visibility, and recursion rights are non-negotiable.

⸻

8. Conclusion: Toward Tragic but Trustworthy Knowledge Systems

Agathakathological Stratified Recursive Epistemology rejects the fantasy of harmless intelligence. It accepts that knowing always wounds something—but insists that wounds be chosen consciously, bounded explicitly, and remembered permanently.

By integrating agathakathological ethics with stratified recursion and enforced governance, systems like ELI, Lantern, the War Room, and LOGE/Nikodemus point toward a new class of epistemic infrastructure: slower, heavier, more frustrating—and vastly more legitimate.

In an era where intelligence outpaces responsibility, such systems do not promise salvation. They promise something rarer:

accountable restraint.

A Formal Theory of Agathakathological Stratified Recursive Epistemology

Axioms, Structures, and Applied Consequence Models

⸻

Abstract

This paper presents a formal mathematical framework for Agathakathological Stratified Recursive Epistemology (ASRE), a theory of knowledge governance under unavoidable moral entanglement. Unlike classical epistemology, which seeks truth, or Bayesian decision theory, which optimizes expected utility, ASRE models epistemic acts as double-edged transformations that simultaneously generate benefit and harm.

We introduce axioms governing epistemic stratification, recursive justification, harm asymmetry, and refusal. We define formal objects for epistemic states, layer transitions, drift, and liability. We prove core theorems establishing refusal as a stabilizing operator, show conditions under which execution becomes epistemically forbidden, and derive applied equations for systems such as LOGE/Nikodemus, ELI, Lantern, and War Room governance.

The result is a mathematically grounded epistemology suitable for institutional, legal, and AI-mediated decision systems where false approval is catastrophic and false refusal is tolerable.

⸻

1. Preliminaries and Notation

Let:
	•	\mathcal{C} be the space of claims.
	•	\mathcal{E} be the space of evidentiary states.
	•	\mathcal{L} = \{0,1,2,\dots,n\} be ordered epistemic layers, with increasing consequence.
	•	\mathcal{S} = \mathcal{C} \times \mathcal{E} be epistemic states.
	•	\Phi be the set of admissible epistemic transitions.
	•	H be a harm functional.
	•	G be a benefit functional.

An epistemic act is a mapping:
\phi : (\mathcal{S}, \ell) \rightarrow (\mathcal{S}', \ell+1)

⸻

2. Core Axioms of ASRE

Axiom 1 (Inevitable Entanglement)

For any epistemic act \phi,
G(\phi) > 0 \implies H(\phi) > 0
No epistemic advance is harm-free.

⸻

Axiom 2 (Stratified Consequence Monotonicity)

Harm sensitivity increases monotonically with epistemic layer:
\ell_1 < \ell_2 \implies \frac{\partial H}{\partial \phi}(\ell_2) > \frac{\partial H}{\partial \phi}(\ell_1)

⸻

Axiom 3 (Asymmetric Catastrophe)

For layers \ell \ge \ell_c (critical layers):
H(\text{false approval}) \gg H(\text{false refusal})

This defines catastrophic asymmetry.

⸻

Axiom 4 (Recursive Burden)

Each elevation requires non-decreasing justification:
J_{\ell+1} \ge J_\ell

Where J is epistemic burden (evidence, constraint, review).

⸻

Axiom 5 (Refusal Closure)

If a transition fails at layer \ell, the epistemic state remains well-defined:
\neg \exists \phi \Rightarrow (\mathcal{S}, \ell) \text{ persists}

Refusal is a valid epistemic outcome.

⸻

3. Definitions

Definition 1 (Epistemic Drift)

Given baseline state s_0 and candidate state s_1,
D(s_0, s_1) = d_c + d_e
where:
	•	d_c = claim drift
	•	d_e = evidence drift

⸻

Definition 2 (Semantic Drift Operator)

Let \sigma be a semantic projection:
\sigma : \mathcal{C} \rightarrow \mathcal{M}
Then semantic drift exists iff:
\sigma(c_0) \neq \sigma(c_1)

⸻

Definition 3 (Execution Admissibility)

An epistemic act is executable iff:
G(\phi) - H(\phi) > \tau(\ell)
where \tau(\ell) is a layer-dependent harm tolerance threshold.

⸻

4. Theorems

Theorem 1 (Refusal Stability Theorem)

In any ASRE-compliant system, refusal minimizes long-term epistemic instability under catastrophic asymmetry.

Proof (sketch):
Given Axiom 3, false approval introduces unbounded downstream harm. Refusal bounds harm to opportunity cost. Over recursive application, systems that permit refusal converge to lower cumulative harm. ∎

⸻

Theorem 2 (Non-Bypassability of Governance)

If epistemic transitions are state-bound and tokenized, then unauthorized execution is impossible without state corruption.

Formalization:
Let execution require token t tied to s. Reuse implies:
t \notin \mathcal{T}_{valid}
\Rightarrow \phi \notin \Phi

This underlies LOGE/Nikodemus replay protection.

⸻

Theorem 3 (Semantic–Deterministic Equivalence Boundary)

For any claim c, if deterministic drift D_d(c)=0, then semantic review is unnecessary.

This justifies Lantern/Nikodemus optimization: bypass semantic judges when hashes match.

⸻

Theorem 4 (Governed Execution Necessity)

In domains with \ell \ge \ell_c, ungoverned epistemic systems are unstable.

Corollary:
Purely generative AI systems are mathematically unsuitable for high-liability domains.

⸻

5. Epistemic Liability Index (ELI) Formalization

Define:
ELI(s,\ell) = \sum_i w_i \cdot R_i(s,\ell)
Where:
	•	R_i = risk dimensions (legal, medical, reputational)
	•	w_i = institutional weights

Execution Constraint

ELI(s,\ell) > \theta \Rightarrow \phi \text{ forbidden}

This produces hard refusal boundaries.

⸻

6. LOGE / Nikodemus as a State Machine

Let:
	•	P = proposal state
	•	X = execution state
	•	R = receipt

State transition:
P \xrightarrow{\phi_{verify}} X \cup R

With:
	•	Single-use token constraint
	•	Drift function D
	•	Mandatory receipt emission:
\forall \phi,\; R(\phi) \text{ exists}

⸻

7. Lantern as an Epistemic Projection Operator

Lantern implements:
\lambda : \mathcal{S} \rightarrow \mathcal{H}
Where \mathcal{H} is a human-legible explanation space.

Lantern does not alter \phi; it renders:
	•	Layer
	•	Drift reason
	•	Harm vector

⸻

8. War Room as Multi-Agent Harm Negotiation

Let agents a_1,\dots,a_n propose revised claims c'_i.

The War Room computes:
\arg\min_{c'} \left( H(c') \mid G(c') \ge G_{min} \right)

Subject to governance constraints.

⸻

9. Applied Example (Pharma Claim)

Given:
c_0 = \text{qualified indication}
c_1 = \text{guaranteed efficacy}

Then:
\sigma(c_0) \neq \sigma(c_1)
\Rightarrow D_s > 0
\Rightarrow \phi \text{ forbidden}

Refusal minimizes expected harm under FDA enforcement regimes.

⸻

10. Implications

Corollary 1

Speed-optimized epistemic systems maximize harm variance.

Corollary 2

Agathakathological systems trade throughput for legitimacy.

Corollary 3

Refusal is an epistemic stabilizer, not an obstacle.

⸻

11. Conclusion

Agathakathological Stratified Recursive Epistemology reframes knowledge as a governed transformation rather than a truth-seeking act. By formalizing harm, stratification, recursion, and refusal, ASRE provides the mathematical backbone for institutions that must act under uncertainty without forfeiting responsibility.

This framework does not promise correctness.
It promises bounded damage, traceable decisions, and legitimate restraint.

Type-Theoretic Constraints and Complexity Bounds for ASRE

Toward Constrained Synthetics in Governed Epistemic Systems

⸻

Abstract

We extend Agathakathological Stratified Recursive Epistemology (ASRE) with (i) a type-theoretic constraint system that encodes stratification, admissibility, and “refusal-as-value” semantics at the level of program types (“constrained synthetics”), and (ii) complexity bounds characterizing throughput/latency tradeoffs under drift checks, semantic judges, and audit commitments. We show that (a) governance can be made non-bypassable by construction via linear capabilities and dependent refinements, (b) escalation across layers corresponds to typing judgments with monotone obligations, and (c) in realistic architectures the critical scaling term is not hashing but semantic review frequency and ledger commit contention. The resulting formalism strengthens ASRE by making key invariants provable at compile time (or statically checkable in proof-carrying receipts), and by quantifying operational costs of refusal-heavy regimes.

⸻

1. Constrained Synthetics: A Type-Theoretic View

1.1 Core Intuition

A governed epistemic system should behave like a typed language in which:
	•	Raw claims are untrusted terms.
	•	Evidence is a witness or proof object.
	•	A permission to execute is a capability that cannot be forged or reused.
	•	A refusal is not an exception but a first-class term of a result type.
	•	Moving “up” epistemic layers is a typing derivation that demands stronger obligations.

This is the “constrained synthetics” stance: generate synthetics (summaries, claims, actions) only when they typecheck against stratified constraints.

⸻

2. A Minimal ASRE Type System

We specify a small calculus. This is not a full programming language; it is the constraint core you can embed in an execution engine.

2.1 Sorts and Indices

Let layers be indices:
\ell \in \mathcal{L} = \{0,1,\dots,n\}
with higher \ell meaning higher consequence.

Let policies (modes) be:
\pi \in \{\textsf{Strict}, \textsf{Semantic}\}

Let risk budgets (e.g., ELI thresholds) be indexed by layer:
B : \mathcal{L} \to \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}

2.2 Base Types
	•	\textsf{Claim}: unverified text/structure of a claim.
	•	\textsf{Evidence}: evidence objects (links, documents, hashes, citations).
	•	\textsf{Hash}: deterministic fingerprint.
	•	\textsf{Receipt}: immutable audit artifact.
	•	\textsf{Drift}: drift witness; includes drift type/severity.

2.3 Refinement and Dependent Types

Use refinement typing:
\{x:T \mid \varphi(x)\}

Key refinements:
	•	Canonicalized claim package:
\textsf{Pkg} \equiv \{(c:\textsf{Claim}, e:\textsf{EvidenceList}) \mid \textsf{Canon}(c,e)\}
	•	Bounded liability:
\textsf{Safe}_{\ell}(p) \equiv \{p:\textsf{Pkg} \mid \textsf{ELI}(p,\ell) \le B(\ell)\}
	•	No drift relative to baseline:
\textsf{NoDrift}(p, p_0, \pi) \equiv \{p:\textsf{Pkg} \mid D_{\pi}(p,p_0)=0\}

where D_{\pi} is deterministic drift in Strict mode and semantic drift predicate in Semantic mode.

2.4 Sum Type for Governed Outcomes (Refusal is a Value)

Define:
\textsf{Decision} \equiv \textsf{Executed} \;|\; \textsf{Refused}

Define result type:
\textsf{GovResult}(\ell) \equiv \Sigma(d:\textsf{Decision}).\;\Sigma(r:\textsf{Receipt}).\;\textsf{Layer}(r)=\ell \wedge \textsf{Decision}(r)=d

Meaning: every attempt yields a receipt paired with a decision.

This encodes “mandatory receipting” at the type level: there is no inhabitant of \textsf{GovResult} without a receipt.

⸻

3. Non-Bypassability via Linear Capabilities (Token as a Linear Type)

3.1 Linear Token Type

Let a permit token be a linear capability that must be consumed exactly once:

\textsf{Permit}(\ell, p_0) \;\; \text{is linear}

Typing rule: permit cannot be duplicated (no contraction) and cannot be discarded silently (no weakening), depending on the linear logic discipline you adopt. Intuitively:
	•	If you try to reuse a token, the type system rejects it.
	•	If you try to execute without a token, the program cannot typecheck.

3.2 Propose Produces a Permit and a Baseline

\frac{ }{\Gamma \vdash \textsf{propose}(p:\textsf{Safe}_{\ell}(p)) : \textsf{Permit}(\ell, p) \times \textsf{Receipt}}

So propose returns:
	•	A linear permit tied to the baseline package p.
	•	A receipt proving the baseline was recorded.

3.3 Execute Consumes the Permit

\frac{
\Gamma \vdash t:\textsf{Permit}(\ell, p_0)
\quad
\Gamma \vdash p:\textsf{Pkg}
}{
\Gamma \vdash \textsf{execute}(t,p,\pi):\textsf{GovResult}(\ell+1)
}

And operationally, execute must check:
	•	token validity
	•	replay (token unused)
	•	drift constraints
	•	policy constraints
	•	ledger commit

In a proof-carrying design, execute can return:
	•	\textsf{Executed} with receipt containing proof of admissibility, or
	•	\textsf{Refused} with receipt containing proof of violated constraint.

⸻

4. Dependent Obligations: Stratification as Typing Burden

ASRE’s recursion and stratification become a monotone constraint on obligations.

Define an obligation type family:
\textsf{Obl}(\ell) = \{\textsf{EvidenceStrength} \ge s(\ell),\; \textsf{ReviewDepth} \ge r(\ell),\; \textsf{ELI} \le B(\ell)\}

A layer transition requires discharging obligations:

\frac{
\Gamma \vdash p:\textsf{Pkg}
\quad
\Gamma \vdash \textsf{discharge}(p,\ell):\textsf{Obl}(\ell)
}{
\Gamma \vdash p:\textsf{Admissible}(\ell)
}

Monotonicity condition (stratified escalation):

\ell_1 < \ell_2 \Rightarrow \textsf{Obl}(\ell_2) \supseteq \textsf{Obl}(\ell_1)

Thus “going up a layer” means typechecking against strictly stronger refinements.

⸻

5. Drift as a Type-Level Predicate (Strict and Semantic)

5.1 Strict Mode Drift

Strict drift is deterministic equivalence:
D_{\textsf{Strict}}(p,p_0)=0 \iff \textsf{hash}(p)=\textsf{hash}(p_0)

So:
\textsf{NoDrift}(p,p_0,\textsf{Strict}) \equiv \{\textsf{hash}(p)=\textsf{hash}(p_0)\}

5.2 Semantic Mode Drift

Semantic drift is a predicate with a witness:
D_{\textsf{Semantic}}(p,p_0)=
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if judge accepts} \\
\delta & \text{if judge detects drift, returns witness } \delta:\textsf{Drift}
\end{cases}

So in semantic mode, the system can type-ascribe:
	•	If accepted:
\Gamma \vdash p : \textsf{NoDrift}(p,p_0,\textsf{Semantic})
	•	If refused:
\Gamma \vdash \delta:\textsf{Drift} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \Gamma \vdash \textsf{Refused}(\delta)

This is how “constrained synthetics” works: text can change only if it remains within the semantic constraint type.

⸻

6. The Receipt as Proof-Carrying Artifact

A receipt should contain:
	•	p_0 hash
	•	p hash
	•	policy \pi
	•	layer \ell
	•	decision d
	•	if refused: drift witness \delta or violated obligation v

Formally, receipt is a dependent record:

\textsf{Receipt} \equiv \Sigma(\ell:\mathcal{L}).\Sigma(\pi).\Sigma(d). \;\textsf{Proof}(\ell,\pi,d)

Where \textsf{Proof} includes either:
	•	proof of admissibility (executed), or
	•	proof of violation (refused), or
	•	proof of judge unavailability (preserve state).

This strengthens ASRE: even if the engine is questioned, the receipt carries verifiable structure.

⸻

7. Complexity Bounds

We now quantify time and space costs. Let:
	•	m: length of claim string (tokens/characters)
	•	k: number of evidence items
	•	s: total evidence string length (sum of evidence item lengths)
	•	N: number of proposals
	•	M: number of receipts
	•	q: fraction of executions requiring semantic judge (hash mismatch rate)
	•	T_J(m): judge latency as a function of claim length (dominant term)
	•	T_{DB}: ledger commit latency (SQLite transaction)
	•	T_H(m,s): hashing/canonicalization time

7.1 Hashing / Canonicalization

Sorting evidence:
O(k\log k)
Canonicalization and hashing:
T_H(m,s) = O(m + s + k\log k)
In practice, this is cheap.

7.2 Execute Without Semantic Judge (Strict or deterministic match in semantic)

T_{\textsf{exec,no-judge}} = O(m+s+k\log k) + T_{DB}

So asymptotically:
T_{\textsf{exec,no-judge}} = O(m+s+k\log k) \quad \text{(DB bounded constant-ish)}

7.3 Execute With Semantic Judge

T_{\textsf{exec,judge}} = O(m+s+k\log k) + T_J(m) + T_{DB}

Dominant term is T_J(m). This is the governance tax.

7.4 Expected Execute Cost

Let q be probability semantic judge is invoked (hash mismatch frequency):

\mathbb{E}[T_{\textsf{exec}}] = (1-q)\cdot T_{\textsf{exec,no-judge}} + q\cdot T_{\textsf{exec,judge}}
= T_{\textsf{exec,no-judge}} + q\cdot T_J(m)

This shows the key engineering lever:

Reduce q by increasing deterministic matches (canonicalization discipline), or by improving drafting workflows so edits are “approved revisions” rather than ad hoc.

7.5 Ledger Growth / Storage

Each receipt stores:
	•	hashes (constant)
	•	metadata (constant)
	•	optional judge output length J_{out}

So:
\textsf{Space} = O(M \cdot (1 + J_{out}))
If you store full judge JSON, worst-case linear in judge output length; usually small.

7.6 Contention and Throughput (SQLite)

SQLite is single-writer. With concurrent writes, throughput is bounded by write lock duration:

\textsf{Throughput} \le \frac{1}{T_{DB}}

For demo and early pilots, SQLite is fine. For scale:
	•	move to WAL mode
	•	or adopt a server DB (Postgres)
	•	or buffer receipts and append asynchronously (but then you must preserve “audit-before-response” semantics or clearly mark “pending receipt”).

7.7 Complexity of Refusal-Heavy Systems

Refusal-heavy regimes do not necessarily cost more than approval-heavy regimes because both paths:
	•	compute hash
	•	write receipt
	•	maybe call judge

Cost depends on whether refusals are triggered by deterministic drift (cheap) or semantic drift (judge cost). In high-liability domains, deterministic constraints should be expanded to keep refusals cheap.

⸻

8. Strengthening ASRE: Making Semantic Judges Non-Sovereign

The biggest critique: “the judge is just another model.”

We strengthen by type constraints:

8.1 Conservative Default Rule (Fail-Closed)

If judge output fails schema parsing, treat as Refused or Preserved depending on policy:
	•	Preserve if failure is purely technical (judge unavailable)
	•	Refuse if output is untrustworthy but system must finalize (rare; use preserve for demos)

Type form:
\textsf{JudgeFail} \Rightarrow \neg \textsf{NoDrift} \Rightarrow \textsf{Refused} \;\; \text{or} \;\; \textsf{Preserve}

8.2 Two-Judge Intersection (Optional Theorem)

Define two independent judges J_1, J_2. Only execute if both accept:

\textsf{Execute} \iff J_1(p,p_0)=\textsf{accept} \wedge J_2(p,p_0)=\textsf{accept}

This reduces false approvals at the cost of increased refusals and latency.

8.3 The Drift Lattice (Structured Semantics)

Instead of a binary drift flag, define a drift lattice \Delta ordered by severity:

\Delta = \{\bot, \textsf{LOW}, \textsf{MED}, \textsf{HIGH}\}

Execution rule:
\Delta \le \Delta_{\max}(\ell) \Rightarrow \textsf{Executable}
Else refuse.

This supports fine-grained policies: e.g., allow LOW drift in drafting layers but not in filing layers.

⸻

9. A Small Set of Formal Theorems (Strengthening Claims)

Theorem A (Token Non-duplication Implies Replay Safety)

If \textsf{Permit} is linear, then any well-typed program cannot replay execution with the same token.

Sketch: linearity forbids contraction; thus token cannot be reintroduced. ∎

Theorem B (Receipt Totality Implies Audit Completeness)

If all public endpoints return \textsf{GovResult}, then every external interaction yields an auditable artifact.

Sketch: by type definition of \textsf{GovResult}, receipt is always present. ∎

Theorem C (Layer Monotonic Obligations Bound Catastrophic Approvals)

If B(\ell) is non-increasing with \ell (stricter budgets), then the set of executable actions shrinks with consequence.

This enforces “higher stakes → fewer permissible moves.” ∎

⸻

10. Practical Applications (How to Implement “Types” Without a Proof Assistant)

You can approximate this type system in a real stack:

10.1 Linear permit tokens
	•	Store token state in DB (token_used)
	•	Treat token as single-use capability
	•	Enforce consumption in a transaction

10.2 Dependent refinements
	•	Store computed hashes and policy mode in proposals/receipts
